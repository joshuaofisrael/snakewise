Which Countries Have No Snakes? Ireland, New Zealand and More

Ireland, New Zealand, Iceland and Greenland have no native land snakes. Cold climates and isolation by sea kept snakes from reaching or surviving there. New Zealand does get occasional venomous sea snakes that drift in on ocean currents, but they cannot live in its cold waters.

Quick facts

  • Ireland: no native snakes. Its only native land reptile is the viviparous (common) lizard. The slow worm found there is a legless lizard, and it is not native.
  • New Zealand: no land snakes. Sea snakes and sea kraits sometimes drift in on currents, but the water is too cold for them to live there.
  • Iceland and Greenland: also snake-free, for the same mix of cold climate and isolation.
  • Why: snakes are ectotherms that rely on outside heat, and they cannot easily cross open sea.

Why does Ireland have no snakes?

The legend credits Saint Patrick. The science points to ice and sea. Scientific American notes that most scientists link Ireland's lack of reptiles to the last ice age, which ended about 11,700 years ago. By the time the ice retreated and the climate warmed enough for cold-blooded animals, Ireland had been cut off from mainland Europe by the sea, so snakes moving north never reached it [1].

Ireland is not empty of reptiles. Ireland's National Biodiversity Data Centre lists six amphibian and reptile species, with the viviparous lizard as the native land reptile and the slow worm as the one non-native species [2]. The slow worm looks like a small snake but is a legless lizard. You can tell lizards from snakes by features such as eyelids, which our snake anatomy guide explains.

Are there snakes in New Zealand?

There are no land snakes in New Zealand. The country's Department of Conservation says sea snakes and sea kraits occasionally arrive on ocean currents or after big storms, but they are non-resident because the waters are too cold for them to survive [3]. Around 12 yellow-bellied sea snakes are seen each year, mostly off the north east coast of the North Island, and live stranded animals are usually unwell from the cold [3].

These visitors are highly venomous, so the department advises keeping people and dogs well away and calling its hotline. It says there is no record of anyone in New Zealand being bitten [3]. Sea snakes are protected there, and it is illegal to kill or keep one without a permit [3].

Which other places have no snakes?

Scientific American lists New Zealand, Iceland and Greenland alongside Ireland as snake-free, and puts it down to the same two forces: cold temperatures and geographic isolation [1]. Snakes cannot make their own body heat, as our snake habitats page describes, so long, cold winters and short summers make it hard for them to survive and breed. Open sea stops new arrivals, unless people bring them.

That last point matters. Islands that are snake-free can gain snakes by accident through shipping and the pet trade. If you ever find a snake somewhere it should not be, keep your distance and report it to the local wildlife authority.

Does "no snakes" mean no risk?

Mostly, on land. But coastal visitors like New Zealand's sea snakes are venomous, so the rule is the same as anywhere else: do not touch, and treat any snake as venomous if you are unsure. FAQ

Did Saint Patrick drive the snakes out of Ireland?

No. There is no evidence Ireland had snakes after the last ice age. Scientists point to the cold and to the sea cutting Ireland off before snakes could spread there [1].

Is the slow worm a snake?

No. It is a legless lizard. In Ireland it is listed as a non-native species [2].

Has anyone been bitten by a sea snake in New Zealand?

New Zealand's Department of Conservation says there is no record of anyone there being bitten [3].

Does Iceland have snakes?

No. Iceland is snake-free, like Greenland and New Zealand [1].
