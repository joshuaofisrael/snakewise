How Venomous Is the Inland Taipan? The World's Most Toxic Snake Venom

The inland taipan (Oxyuranus microlepidotus) of outback Australia has the most toxic venom of any snake tested: a lethal dose in mice of about 0.01 mg per kg, with around 17 mg injected per bite. Its venom attacks nerves and blood clotting, yet bites on people are extremely rare, with only about a dozen documented cases.

Quick facts

  • Who: the inland taipan or fierce snake (Oxyuranus microlepidotus), an elapid of outback Australia.
  • Venom strength: a lethal dose in mice of about 0.01 mg per kg of body weight, the most toxic snake venom measured (Morrison et al., 1983).
  • Venom per bite: about 17.3 mg on average in field biting tests (Morrison et al., 1983).
  • What it does: blocks nerve signals to muscles and stops blood from clotting properly (Hodgson et al., 2007; Weinstein et al., 2017).
  • Bites on people: very rare. Only about a dozen well-documented cases, most of them involving captive snakes (Weinstein et al., 2025).

How venomous is the inland taipan?

By the standard lab test it is the most venomous snake in the world. Toxicologists compare venoms using the LD50, the dose that kills half of a group of test mice. The lower the number, the stronger the venom. In a 1983 study, Morrison and colleagues reported a mouse LD50 of 0.01 mg/kg for the inland taipan and called it "the world's most venomous snake". Later researchers agree: Hodgson, Dal Belo and Rowan (2007) open their paper with the line "The inland taipan is the world's most venomous snake."

The same team also measured how much venom the snake actually injects. In field biting experiments, an inland taipan delivered an average of 17.3 mg of venom per strike. Dividing that by the lethal dose gave an "injected mass to LD50" ratio of 1,730, the highest recorded for any snake they tested (Morrison et al., 1983). Mouse results don't translate neatly into human deaths.

What is in inland taipan venom?

A mix of proteins that attack the nerves, the blood and the blood vessels. A 2025 proteomics study compared inland and coastal taipan venoms and found nine protein families in both, including phospholipase A2 enzymes, three-finger toxins, natriuretic peptides and prothrombin activators (Paz et al., 2025). The inland taipan's venom had a wider range of three-finger toxins, and these made up about 33% of the venom compared with 9% in the coastal taipan. Phospholipase A2s made up about 47%, against 66% in the coastal species.

The best-studied single toxin is paradoxin, a "beta-neurotoxin" that works at the nerve ending. In nerve-muscle preparations from chicks and mice, paradoxin first made nerves release extra bursts of acetylcholine and then shut the release down, so muscles stopped responding. Hodgson and colleagues (2007) concluded it is "one of the most potent, if not the most potent" beta-neurotoxin known. The whole venom also caused an immediate drop in blood pressure and cardiovascular collapse in anaesthetised rats at 50 micrograms per kg injected into a vein (Bell, Sutherland & Hodgson, 1998). Is a baby inland taipan less dangerous? Not by much. When Tan, Ponnudurai and Mirtschin (1993) compared venom from juvenile and adult snakes, the lethal dose, enzyme activity and clotting effects were not significantly different,.

Why is its venom so strong?

Probably because of what it eats. The Australian Museum reports that wild inland taipans appear to feed entirely on mammals, especially the long-haired rat (Rattus villosissimus), plus house mice and small marsupials called dasyurids. A taipan usually corners prey in a burrow or soil crack and bites several times in quick succession. Because the venom acts so fast, the snake can hold on rather than letting go and waiting, which lowers the risk of being injured by a struggling rat in a tight space. Fast-acting venom that is extremely toxic to mammals fits that hunting style, although this link is an interpretation rather than a tested result.

Has anyone been bitten by an inland taipan?

Yes, but very few people. A 1984 report described a bite from a three-week-old inland taipan as only the second recorded case, and noted that taipan antivenom worked (Mirtschin, Crowe & Thomas, 1984). By 2017 there had been 11 well-documented envenomings, only two of them by wild snakes (Weinstein et al., 2017). Most victims developed clotting failure (defibrinating coagulopathy) and paralysis.

The 2017 case shows how serious a bite can be. A man trying to photograph a wild snake about 1.5 m long in remote South Australia developed drowsiness, blurred vision, drooping eyelids and trouble swallowing and speaking. He was intubated during the air retrieval, given polyvalent and taipan antivenom, and recovered after three days of intensive care, though one eye's movement stayed impaired for about a week (Weinstein et al., 2017). In a 2025 case, a 22-year-old keeper bitten by a captive inland taipan in Florida passed out within about five minutes, developed severe clotting problems and cranial nerve palsies, and was discharged after 48 hours following antivenom (Weinstein et al., 2025). Those authors count about 12 formally documented cases in total.

Brown snakes, not taipans, cause most of Australia's snakebite deaths. The Australian Snakebite Project recorded 23 deaths from 2005 to 2015, attributed to brown snakes (17), tiger snakes (4) and unknown snakes (2) (Johnston et al., 2017). If anyone is bitten by any snake, call emergency services at once; our snake safety guide explains what to do.

Where does the inland taipan live?

In the Channel Country of south-western Queensland and north-eastern South Australia (Australian Museum). It lives on cracking-clay floodplains with sparse shrubs and shelters in deep soil cracks and mammal burrows. It is active mainly in the morning and changes colour with the seasons, turning darker in winter and paler in summer. FAQ

What is the most venomous snake in the world?

The inland taipan (Oxyuranus microlepidotus) of Australia, with a mouse LD50 of about 0.01 mg/kg (Morrison et al., 1983).

How much venom does an inland taipan inject?

About 17.3 mg per strike on average in field biting experiments (Morrison et al., 1983).

Has anyone died from an inland taipan bite?

Bites are very rare, with about 12 formally documented cases. The patients in recent published case reports survived after antivenom and hospital care (Weinstein et al., 2017; 2025). Any bite is a medical emergency.

Is there antivenom for the inland taipan?

Yes. Taipan antivenom and Australian polyvalent antivenom have been used successfully (Mirtschin et al., 1984; Weinstein et al., 2017).

What does the inland taipan eat?

Small to medium mammals, especially the long-haired rat, plus house mice and small dasyurids (Australian Museum).
