Study: Snakebites May Envenom Up to 7 Million People a Year

A new global estimate suggests snakebite is far deadlier than once thought: at least 2.1 million envenomings and 274,000 deaths a year, and possibly up to 7 million envenomings and 513,000 deaths.

What did the new study find?

A team led by Dileepa Ediriweera and David Lalloo published a fresh global estimate of snakebite harm in PLOS Medicine on 25 August 2026. Their low estimate is at least 2.1 million snake envenomings and 274,000 deaths every year. Their high estimate, which adjusts for cases that never reach official records, is up to 7 million envenomings and 513,000 deaths a year.

The authors also report that sub-Saharan Africa accounts for about 45% of global envenomings and deaths, with an incidence nearly three times that seen in South Asia. Low-income countries had the highest rates of both bites with venom and deaths.

Why are the numbers so much higher than before?

The same research group published the first modelled global estimate in 2008. That study put the yearly toll at at least 421,000 envenomings and 20,000 deaths, and possibly as many as 1,841,000 envenomings and 94,000 deaths.

For the 2026 update, the team reviewed studies in any language published up to 31 March 2025 and used envenoming data from 79 countries. Many countries keep no count of snakebites, so the researchers used geostatistical models. These combine the real data points with climate, snake species richness and social and economic conditions to estimate rates for every country, including places with no direct records. Comparing hospital figures with community surveys let them estimate how many cases go unreported.

The authors say plainly that this has limits. Data are scarce and uneven in many countries, and the estimates depend on modelling assumptions where nobody has measured bites directly.

What about people who survive?

The study did not model long-term disability directly. In their discussion the authors give a rough figure instead: if amputations and other permanent physical disabilities happen at about three times the number of deaths, at least 825,000 people a year would be left with severe physical disabilities. They add that psychological harm after snakebite comes on top of that.

Why it matters

The World Health Organization aims to halve deaths and disability from snakebite by 2030. Writing for The Conversation, the lead author argues that country-level maps can help governments decide where to stock antivenom, train rural health workers and improve transport to clinics.
