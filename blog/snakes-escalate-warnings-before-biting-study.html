Study: Snakes Escalate Their Warning Sounds Before Biting, Across 73 Species

A hissing or rattling snake is usually telling you something, and a new study suggests it is also telling you how close it is to biting. Researchers who tested 220 snakes from 73 species found that warning sounds grew longer and generally louder as the threat increased, while bites came mainly at the highest threat level, as a last resort.

The news in brief

  • What: a comparative study of how snakes move from warning signals to biting as danger rises.
  • Who: Nicolas Van Zele, Olivier Lourdais and colleagues, from French research centres including CNRS's Centre d'Etudes Biologiques de Chizé and Université Claude Bernard Lyon 1, with co-authors from UCLouvain in Belgium.
  • Where published: Functional Ecology, online 6 October 2026, open access under a CC BY-NC 4.0 licence. The data are deposited on Figshare.
  • Scale: 220 individual snakes from 73 species, covering four ways of making warning sounds.
  • Main result: warnings escalate with threat, biting is mostly a last resort, and how a warning escalates depends on how the sound is made.

What did the researchers test?

Many snakes make a noise when threatened. The study grouped them into four "modes" of sound production: two the authors call simple (hissing, and vibrating the tail) and two they call highly specialised (rattling, and rubbing scales together). Each snake was taken through three phases of rising threat: a low-threat phase with no predator-like stimulus aimed at it, a medium-threat phase with visual stimulation, and a high-threat phase that combined visual and touch stimulation. The analysis accounted for how closely related the species are, so the patterns are not just a quirk of a few related snakes.

What did they find?

The results fit what biologists call the threat sensitivity hypothesis: an animal should scale its defence to the danger it faces, because strong defences cost energy and carry their own risks. Across species, snakes put more into their acoustic warnings as the threat rose. Their signals became longer and generally more intense, and biting happened mainly in the high-threat phase. In the authors' words, "biting serves as a last-resort tactic."

The four sound modes did not escalate in the same way:

  • Signal length: rattling and scale rubbing lengthened more steeply with threat than hissing did, and tail vibration fell in between.
  • Loudness: hissing kept getting more intense through every phase; rattling rose overall but levelled off between the medium and high phases; scale rubbing rose at first and then levelled off; tail vibration showed no significant change.
  • Body size: larger snakes were less likely to bite or otherwise physically confront the threat.

The authors conclude that snake defence is "a dynamic sequence" rather than a fixed reaction, and that the way a warning escalates depends on the mechanism that produces it.

How does this fit with earlier work?

Several of the same researchers published a single-species study earlier in 2026 on the nose-horned viper (Vipera ammodytes) in Proceedings of the Royal Society B. Using the same three-step rise in threat, they found that hissing grew longer and louder as threat increased, that bites came only in the visual and combined phases, and that snakes which hissed more often and more intensely also bit more. They described this as evidence that hissing can be an honest signal of how ready a viper is to defend itself. The new paper extends that idea from one viper to dozens of species and several kinds of sound.

What does this mean for people who meet snakes?

The study was about how snakes respond to rising threat in a test setting, not a field guide to human encounters, so it is best read as background rather than a rule. Still, it supports the familiar advice on our snake safety page: a snake that is hissing, rattling or buzzing is warning you, and the right response is to stop, step back and give it space. Most bites happen when people try to catch, kill or move snakes. Not every species makes warning sounds, and colour or sound alone is never a safe way to decide whether a snake is venomous; our venomous or not ID guide covers lookalikes by region.
