Study: Individual Rattlesnakes Are Consistent in How Much Venom They Use

Not every rattlesnake bites the same way. A new study tested 48 southern Pacific rattlesnakes three times each and found that individual snakes were consistent in how readily they bit and how much venom they released, even after allowing for body size. Larger snakes released more venom.

The news in brief

  • What: a test of whether individual rattlesnakes are consistent in how readily they bite and how much venom they release in defensive bites.
  • Who: Chelsea E. Martin and William K. Hayes, Department of Earth and Biological Sciences, Loma Linda University, California.
  • Where published: Frontiers in Ethology, volume 5, article 1918065, published 8 October 2026. The authors summarised the work for Phys.org (Science X Dialog) on 21 October 2026.
  • Snakes studied: 48 southern Pacific rattlesnakes (Crotalus oreganus helleri): 35 adults that were wild-caught in southern California and long kept in captivity, and 13 captive-born subadults.
  • Main finding: all five measured bite behaviours were repeatable for each snake across three trials, including how much venom it released, even after allowing for body size.

What did the researchers do?

Each snake went through three standard venom extractions, about two weeks apart. A snake was first guided into a clear plastic tube with its head out, and the team counted any defensive strikes during that step. The snake was then offered the edge of a beaker covered with a thin film and given 30 seconds to bite it voluntarily. The venom glands were not squeezed. Slow-motion video was used to count the venom pulses from each fang, and the venom collected was measured.

That gave five measures per trial: strikes while being tubed, whether the snake bit, the number of venom pulses, the volume of venom, and the venom released per pulse.

What did they find?

All five measures were significantly repeatable: snakes that struck, bit or released more venom in one trial tended to do the same in the others. The repeatability scores, on a scale from 0 to 1, were moderate, at roughly 0.26 to 0.47. For venom volume the figure was 0.41, and the number of venom pulses was the most consistent at 0.47.

Size mattered, as expected. Larger snakes released more venom, and more pulses meant more venom. But the differences between individual snakes remained after the researchers accounted for both. The authors interpret this as venom use having a consistent, individual component, the kind of stable difference biologists study as animal personality.

Two other details stand out. Nearly half of the snakes (23 of 48) never struck while being tubed, yet every snake bit the beaker in at least one trial. And strikes showed a trend toward increasing by the third trial rather than fading, which the authors suggest may mean the snakes were not getting used to the procedure.

Why it matters

Rattlesnakes can adjust how much venom they use, a behaviour known as venom metering. This study adds that individuals also differ from one another in a consistent way. The authors note that the southern Pacific rattlesnake causes most medically significant snakebites in southern California, and that larger snakes typically inject more venom. Their conclusion is that bite risk may vary not only with a snake's size but from one individual to another.

The size result also fits a common myth we cover on our myths vs facts page: the idea that baby snakes are more dangerous than adults. In this study, larger rattlesnakes released more venom.

The authors themselves point out the limits. The snakes were restrained and tested in a lab, which is not the same as a free-ranging snake striking in the wild. The adults had been kept in captivity for 2 to 10 or more years. Testing ran over only a few weeks. They call for studies of unrestrained and free-ranging snakes to see whether the same differences hold.

The most practical point comes from the researchers too: no rattlesnake should be approached or handled on the assumption that it will behave predictably. Some snakes may be less inclined to bite, but consistent differences do not make any particular snake safe. Keep your distance, and see our snake safety guide for what to do if you meet one.
