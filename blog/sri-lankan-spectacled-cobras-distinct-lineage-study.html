Study: Sri Lanka's Spectacled Cobras Form a Distinct Genetic Lineage

The spectacled cobra (Naja naja) looks and behaves differently from place to place across South Asia, but a new genetic study finds that cobras across mainland India are remarkably similar underneath. The exception is Sri Lanka: the island's cobras form their own genetic lineage, which the researchers estimate split off around two million years ago. They stop short of calling it a separate species.

The news in brief

  • What: a study of the evolutionary history of the spectacled cobra, one of the snakes behind most serious snakebites in India.
  • Who: Paulomi Dam Kanunjna, Kartik Sunagar and colleagues; Sunagar is an evolutionary biologist at the Indian Institute of Science's Centre for Ecological Sciences.
  • Where published: Molecular Phylogenetics and Evolution, volume 223 (2026), article 108654.
  • Scale: 67 cobras sampled across major Indian regions, nine mitochondrial and nuclear DNA markers, combined with published sequences from neighbouring countries.
  • Main result: no clear regional lineages within mainland India, but a distinct Sri Lankan lineage.

Why were scientists unsure about this cobra?

The spectacled cobra ranges across India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan and Sri Lanka, and populations differ in colour, hood markings and venom. Early naturalists split it into regional forms. In 1939 the Sri Lankan zoologist P. E. P. Deraniyagala described an island form with extra eye-like hood markings, later lumped back into Naja naja.

The debate reopened in 2022, when a genetic study proposed treating Sri Lankan cobras as their own species. In 2023 herpetologists Wolfgang Wüster and Frank Tillack challenged that, arguing the evidence leaned too heavily on mitochondrial DNA and limited sampling, and designated a Sri Lankan museum specimen as the official name-bearing type of Naja naja. A big gap remained: very few Indian cobras had ever been sequenced, even though India covers most of the species' range.

What did the new study find?

Mainland cobras are one big, mixed population. Across India the team found minimal sequence divergence and extensive sharing of the same genetic variants, despite the visible regional differences. The authors describe the mainland snakes as "a largely panmictic assemblage", meaning they behave genetically like one freely interbreeding population. They suggest strong dispersal, a generalist lifestyle and historical movement of snakes by people as likely reasons. That contrasts with other wide-ranging South Asian snakes, such as the banded krait and the king cobra, which do split into distinct regional lineages.

Sri Lanka's cobras stand apart. The island population formed a distinct genetic group. The researchers estimate it separated from mainland cobras around two million years ago, around the Pliocene–Pleistocene boundary. Their suggested explanation is sea level: when seas were low, Sri Lanka was joined to southern India by land; when they rose, the island's cobras were cut off. Both the study and the reporting on it treat this as a plausible explanation, not a proven cause.

An African origin. The analysis also supports an African origin for the cobra genus Naja, with the ancestors of the spectacled cobra reaching South Asia via the Middle East during the Miocene.

So is the Sri Lankan cobra a new species?

Not according to this study. The authors say Sri Lankan and mainland cobras still fall within one species, and that genetic distinctiveness alone does not settle species status. Speaking to Mongabay, Sunagar said the team "haven't hurried" to name a separate species because that needs a different scientific approach and more work. The authors call for denser sampling, especially in the areas where island and mainland populations might once have met.

Why it matters

Knowing which populations are genuinely separate helps scientists classify, study and conserve them, and the spectacled cobra is a medically important elapid. Earlier research, including work by Sunagar's group, has found that cobra venom varies from region to region, so the study is a reminder that a snake can look and even act different across its range without splitting into separate lineages. It is also a good example of island geography shaping evolution, a theme that runs through our habitats page.

If you live where spectacled cobras occur, the practical advice doesn't change: give any snake space, don't try to handle it, and treat any bite as a medical emergency. Our safety page explains what to do, and our South Asia ID guide covers telling venomous snakes from harmless lookalikes.
