Why Do Snakes Flick Their Tongues? How the Forked Tongue Works

Snakes flick their tongues to smell. Each flick picks up chemicals from the air and ground and delivers them to the vomeronasal organs in the roof of the mouth, and the forked tips let a snake sample two spots at once to tell which way a scent trail runs.

Quick facts

  • A snake flicks its tongue to smell. The tongue picks up chemicals from the air and the ground and carries them into the mouth (Kubie, 1978).
  • The chemicals are read by the vomeronasal organs, a pair of sense organs that open through the roof of the mouth (Filoramo & Schwenk, 2009).
  • The fork lets a snake sample two points at once and tell which side a scent trail is on (Schwenk, 1994).
  • Snakes flick faster when a scent is stronger, after striking prey, and they even change their flicks in water (Kubie, 1978; Chiszar et al., 1993; Ryerson & Schwenk, 2022).
  • The tongue cannot sting or poison you. Venom comes from glands and fangs, not the tongue (see our venom and constriction guide).

Why do snakes flick their tongues?

Snakes flick their tongues to collect smells. Each flick touches the air or the ground and picks up tiny amounts of chemicals left by prey, predators and other snakes. When the tongue goes back in, those chemicals are passed to the vomeronasal organs in the roof of the mouth, which work like a second nose. So a flicking tongue is a snake sniffing, not tasting the air in the way we taste food, and not a threat.

Lab work on garter snakes shows how central this is. In a Y-shaped maze, all nine garter snakes tested learned to follow an earthworm-extract trail, reaching at least 8 correct choices out of 10, and each snake had its own steady tongue-flick rate that rose with how fast it moved (Kubie & Halpern, 1975). In a later study, snakes could still follow trails that had dried, but they could not follow a trail placed just out of the tongue's reach under a perforated floor (Kubie, 1978). If the tongue cannot touch the scent, the trail is lost.

Why is a snake's tongue forked?

The fork is a direction finder. Herpetologist Kurt Schwenk brought together evidence from anatomy, nerve pathways and behaviour to argue that the forked tongue works as a "chemical edge detector". Because the two tips touch the ground at two points at the same moment, a snake can compare them and instantly tell whether it is on a trail or drifting off one side (Schwenk, 1994).

The same review found that forked tongues evolved at least twice, and possibly four times, among lizards and snakes, and that at higher levels of the family tree they always go with an active, wide-searching way of hunting. Schwenk suggested that refining this trail-following tool, for finding both prey and mates, may be part of why advanced snakes became so successful (Schwenk, 1994). Garter snake males, for example, follow female scent trails in the breeding season, which we cover in why garter snakes form mating balls.

Where do the smells go after a tongue flick?

A snake's tongue tips do not simply poke straight into its nose. The vomeronasal organs are reached only through small ducts opening in the front of the palate, so chemicals have to be moved from the tongue tips to those openings (Filoramo & Schwenk, 2009). An older idea, based on advanced snakes, was that soft pads on the floor of the mouth carry the chemicals up. Filoramo and Schwenk tested how this works in lizards with unforked tongues and proposed a different, hydraulic system: when the mouth closes, the tongue is squeezed in a snug chamber and acts like a piston, pushing chemical-carrying fluid towards the organ openings almost instantly. Their results ruled out the older idea for those lizards; how widely each mechanism applies in snakes is still being studied (Filoramo & Schwenk, 2009).

How does a snake's tongue move so fast?

Snake and lizard tongues have no bones. They are muscular hydrostats, like an elephant's trunk or our own tongue, and they lengthen and change shape by squeezing muscle against fluid-filled tissue. A 2025 review found that chemosensory tongue-flicking in all lizards and snakes is driven by this kind of hydrostatic lengthening, and that snakes have a unique, rapid, oscillating flick that matches the special arrangement of muscle fibres in their tongues (Schwenk, 2025). You can read more about other unusual snake body parts on our snake anatomy page.

Do snakes flick their tongues differently in water?

Yes. High-speed video of northern water snakes (Nerodia sipedon) showed that flicks on land are long, with several oscillations, a large reach and high speeds. Underwater, flicks are much shorter, reach less far and oscillate fewer times. At the water's surface, when frogs were nearby, the snakes simply tapped their tongue tips on the surface, which the authors think helps them pick up frog scents that travel along the water's surface (Ryerson & Schwenk, 2022).

Why does a snake flick its tongue more after striking?

Many vipers strike prey, let it go, and then track it while the venom works. After a strike, rattlesnakes switch into what researchers call strike-induced chemosensory searching, a burst of raised tongue-flicking that is a typical part of how rattlesnakes hunt (Chiszar et al., 1993). In one experiment, only rodent blood placed on prairie rattlesnakes' lips raised their tongue-flick rate, while water and skin cues did not, which suggests that detecting blood during a strike may help switch the search on (Chiszar et al., 1993).

Scent also helps ambush hunters choose where to wait. Captive-raised timber rattlesnakes that had never met wild prey explored chemical cues with tongue-flicks and took up their ambush posture more often towards cues from prey found where their parents came from than towards other species' scents (Clark, 2004). Rattlesnakes also use heat to hunt, as explained in can snakes see heat?

Does a snake flicking its tongue mean it is about to bite?

No. Tongue-flicking on its own is just a snake checking its surroundings, and snakes do it when calm, curious or alert. It does not tell you a snake is friendly either. Any wild snake should be left alone and given space, and you should never judge whether a snake is safe from its behaviour. See our snake safety guide and the venomous-or-not ID guide (venom labels may be wrong, so always treat a snake as dangerous). Researchers even use tongue-flick counts as a measure of how interested a snake is in a scent, for example in studies of which prey scents captive-bred garter snakes prefer before they have ever met that prey (Carrillo et al., 2026).

FAQ

Can a snake's tongue hurt you?

No. A snake's tongue is a soft sense organ for smelling. Venom is made in glands and delivered by fangs, not by the tongue.

Do snakes smell with their tongue or their nose?

Both. Snakes have nostrils for normal smell, and the tongue brings chemicals to the vomeronasal organs in the roof of the mouth (Filoramo & Schwenk, 2009).

Why are snake tongues forked?

The two tips sample two places at once, so a snake can tell which way a scent trail runs and stay on it (Schwenk, 1994).

Can snakes follow a trail without touching it?

In lab tests, garter snakes could follow dried earthworm trails but not trails kept out of reach of the tongue (Kubie, 1978).
