Study: Venomous Snakes Are More Likely to Stand Out From Their Surroundings

A new study of 458 snake species finds that venomous snakes tend to be more eye-catching against their own natural backgrounds than harmless snakes do, which supports the idea that some advertise danger with colour. But plenty of venomous snakes are still drab, so colour on its own is no safe way to tell a dangerous snake from a harmless one.

News · Published 15 October 2026 · Last reviewed 15 October 2026

The news in brief

  • What: researchers at the University of Galway tested whether venomous snakes are more conspicuous, measured against the habitats they actually live in.
  • Where published: the Journal of Animal Ecology, online 4 October 2026 (Duclaux, Dugon & Healy).
  • Scale: images of 458 snake species, gathered from the citizen-science platform iNaturalist.
  • Main result: venomous species were significantly more conspicuous in context than non-venomous species.
  • Surprise: how strong or how painful a snake's venom is did not predict how conspicuous it was, with the data the team used.

What did the study look at?

Many animals use bold colours to warn predators that they are a bad meal. Biologists call this aposematism; wasps, poison dart frogs and coral snakes are textbook examples. The problem the Galway team set out to fix is that a snake can look striking in a museum drawer or on a plain background and still be well camouflaged where it actually lives. So instead of judging colour in isolation, they compared each species with its natural surroundings.

To do that at scale, they collected photographs of 458 species taken in the wild through iNaturalist, and built a new measure of how much each species stands out from its own background. They then checked that measure against whether a species is venomous, how potent its venom is, and whether its venom is known for causing pain.

What did they find?

Venomous snakes were, on average, significantly more contextually conspicuous than non-venomous snakes. In the university's announcement, lead researcher Amy Duclaux said that "some venomous snakes appear to advertise that they're dangerous", and that red and yellow were particularly effective at helping snakes stand out against their backgrounds.

The authors were careful about what the data do not show. They found no link between conspicuousness and venom potency, or between conspicuousness and how pain-inducing a venom is. In their words, those traits "may not be important drivers" of warning colouration in snakes. Being venomous at all seems to matter more than how dangerous the venom is.

Why it matters

This is one of the largest tests yet of the idea that venom and warning colours evolved together across snakes as a whole, rather than in a few well-studied groups like coral snakes. It also shows how photos taken by ordinary naturalists can power real evolutionary research: Dr Kevin Healy, who heads the university's Macroecology Lab, noted that wild photos are essential for seeing how colours appear "in the environments where they evolved".

Does this mean bright snakes are venomous?

No. The finding is a statistical tendency across hundreds of species, not a rule for any single snake. The university's own summary notes that many venomous snakes are not brightly coloured, and the study found venom potency doesn't track with colour either. On top of that, some harmless snakes closely resemble venomous ones, which is exactly what our Lookalike Lab game is built around. If you meet a snake in the wild, keep your distance and don't try to identify it by colour alone.
