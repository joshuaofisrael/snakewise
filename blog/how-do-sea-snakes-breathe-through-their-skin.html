How Do Sea Snakes Breathe Through Their Skin?

Yes, partly. Yellow-bellied sea snakes absorb some oxygen from seawater through their skin and release most of their carbon dioxide the same way. In lab tests the skin supplied up to a third of resting oxygen needs, helping these snakes make dives of more than three hours, although they still surface to breathe air.

Published 15 October 2026 · Last reviewed 15 October 2026

Quick facts

  • Who: the yellow-bellied or pelagic sea snake (Hydrophis platurus, long known as Pelamis platurus), a venomous elapid that is black above, yellow below and has a flat, paddle-shaped tail.
  • Skin breathing: in lab tests, oxygen taken in through the skin reached up to 33% of the snake's resting oxygen use, and carbon dioxide released through the skin reached up to 94% of that figure (Graham, 1974).
  • Diving: tracked snakes dived to 50 m and stayed down for up to 213 minutes (Rubinoff, Graham & Motta, 1986).
  • Range: the most widely distributed snake on Earth, found across the tropical Indian and Pacific Oceans (Reptile Database). IUCN status: Least Concern.

Can sea snakes breathe through their skin?

Yes, partly. This is cutaneous respiration: dissolved oxygen diffuses from seawater through the skin into the blood, and carbon dioxide diffuses out. The snake must still surface to fill its lung, but its skin works as a second, slower gas exchanger the whole time it is underwater.

In Jeffrey Graham's classic study, while snakes were diving or swimming at the surface, their skin took oxygen from the water at rates up to 33% of their total resting (standard) oxygen uptake. It released carbon dioxide into the water at rates up to 94% of their resting oxygen consumption (Graham, 1974).

Those are best-case figures. When Heatwole and Seymour (1975) measured six Indo-Pacific sea snake species and the marine file snake, oxygen uptake through the skin ranged from 0 to 22% of the total. They also concluded that all of the carbon dioxide a snake produces may be lost through the skin. So the skin's biggest job is clearing carbon dioxide, with oxygen as a smaller bonus. Why does skin breathing help a diving snake?

Graham (1974) listed four benefits. Skin breathing helps the snake while it feeds, adds extra oxygen during dives, and releases carbon dioxide so the blood doesn't turn acidic (respiratory acidosis). It also gives dissolved nitrogen and other inert gases a way to balance with the water, which may lower the risk of decompression sickness, also called "the bends" or caisson disease.

But skin works both ways: blood richer in oxygen than the water could lose oxygen through it. In studies of other sea snakes, Seymour and Webster (1975) found that a large share of blood bypasses the lung, which keeps blood oxygen fairly low even when the lung is full. They suggested this shunting reduces oxygen loss through the skin and may also help prevent decompression sickness after deep dives.

How long can a yellow-bellied sea snake stay underwater?

For hours. Rubinoff, Graham and Motta (1986) fitted 15 snakes in the Gulf of Panamá with pressure-sensing ultrasonic tags. The snakes spent an average of 87% of the tracking time underwater (up to 99.9%), dived as deep as 50 m and stayed down for up to 213 minutes. Many dives were longer than the snakes' estimated oxygen stores should allow, yet the snakes dived again and again without long recovery breaks at the surface. The authors suggested two explanations, which may work together: a lowered metabolic rate and extra oxygen taken in through the skin.

When Cook and Brischoux (2014) reanalysed the published dive profiles, they found mostly S-shaped dives averaging about 55 minutes. During a long phase of slow ascent the snake floats without effort, neutrally buoyant, while its lung slowly deflates. They argue the snakes dive mainly to escape rough surface water, and save energy by drifting in cooler layers. See snake habitats.

Is the lung still important?

Very. The large lung handles both gas exchange and buoyancy. Resting snakes float high; on long dives they reach neutral or negative buoyancy as depth squeezes the lung and oxygen is used without an equal amount of carbon dioxide replacing it (Graham, Gee & Robison, 1975). This fits neatly with skin breathing: carbon dioxide that leaves through the skin isn't sent back into the lung.

Do other snakes breathe through their skin?

Many aquatic snakes exchange some gas through their skin, but the amount tracks how aquatic they are. Heatwole and Seymour (1978) found that four fully marine Hydrophis sea snakes had high skin oxygen uptake, at 1.50 to 1.94 ml per 100 g per hour. Sea kraits (Laticauda), which come ashore, and the amphibious dog-faced water snake Cerberus rhynchops usually managed only 0.45 to 0.74. Compare species on our snake species guide.

Skin breathing doesn't mean thin skin. Across 192 snakes of 44 species, many (but not all) sea snakes had skin about 50% thicker than land and amphibious snakes, thanks to extra collagen that likely guards against scrapes and fouling organisms (Shine et al., 2019).

Can sea snakes drink seawater?

No. Field studies in Costa Rica show that yellow-bellied sea snakes become dehydrated at sea during the dry season and don't drink seawater. They rehydrate by drinking from a layer of fresh water that forms on the ocean surface during heavy rain, and they may stay dehydrated for six to seven months at a time (Lillywhite et al., 2014).

Is the yellow-bellied sea snake dangerous?

It is a venomous elapid, so never handle one, including snakes washed up on a beach. Read our snake safety guide and venomous or not ID guide, and see venom and constriction for how snake venom works.

FAQ

Can sea snakes breathe underwater?

Not the way fish do. They must surface to breathe air into their lung, but while submerged their skin takes in some oxygen from the water and releases carbon dioxide into it (Graham, 1974).

How long can a yellow-bellied sea snake hold its breath?

Tracked snakes in the Gulf of Panamá stayed underwater for up to 213 minutes, about 3.5 hours, and dived to 50 m (Rubinoff, Graham & Motta, 1986).

How much oxygen do sea snakes get through their skin?

Up to about a third of resting oxygen needs in yellow-bellied sea snakes (Graham, 1974), and 0 to 22% across six other sea snake species and the marine file snake (Heatwole & Seymour, 1975). Carbon dioxide loss through the skin is much larger.

Is the yellow-bellied sea snake endangered?

No. It is listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List and is the most widely distributed snake species.
