Can Snakes Reproduce Without Mating? Meet the Brahminy Blind Snake

Yes. The Brahminy blind snake (Indotyphlops braminus), also called the flowerpot snake, is the only snake species known to reproduce entirely without mating. All known individuals are female, eggs develop without sperm, and the species has hitchhiked in potting soil to every continent except Antarctica.

Published 12 October 2026 · Last reviewed 12 October 2026

Quick facts

  • Short answer: yes — one snake species, the Brahminy blind snake (Indotyphlops braminus), reproduces without mating. Every known individual is female, and daughters hatch from unfertilised eggs (obligate parthenogenesis).
  • Why it works: the species is triploid (three sets of chromosomes, typically 42). Genome studies show three ancestral subgenomes and point to a meiosis workaround called premeiotic endoreplication.
  • Size and look: a tiny burrower about 10 to 15 cm long, often mistaken for a shiny earthworm; harmless to people.
  • Where: native to South Asia, now found on every continent except Antarctica after hitchhiking in soil and flower pots.

Can snakes reproduce without mating?

For almost every snake species, the answer is no: eggs or live young need a male. The exception is the Brahminy blind snake, also called the flowerpot snake. It is the only snake known to reproduce entirely by parthenogenesis — females lay eggs that develop into more females without sperm. Large museum series found no males, and later work confirmed that populations are all-female clones.

That makes this species a rare natural experiment. Most vertebrates need two parents. This one does not, and it has used that trick to become one of the most widely introduced reptiles on Earth. You can meet it alongside other standouts on our species page.

What is the Brahminy blind snake?

Indotyphlops braminus (older names include Ramphotyphlops braminus) belongs to the blind snakes, Scolecophidia — burrowers with reduced eyes that mainly sense light. Adults are usually about 10 to 15 cm long. They live in soil, leaf litter and rotting wood, hunting ants and termites. Because they travel in potting soil, gardeners sometimes find them in flower pots. The Reptile Database and genome papers note introductions across Asia, Africa, the Americas, Australia and many islands; Antarctica is the only continent without a record. How do we know it needs no males?

Several lines of evidence stack up.

  • No males in samples. Surveys of large series, including Nussbaum's 1980 Seychelles work and earlier notes by McDowell, found only females. Reports of "males" have generally been other blind-snake species misidentified.
  • Triploid karyotype. In 1987, Wynn, Cole and Gardner karyotyped specimens from Hawaii, the Seychelles and south Florida. They counted 42 chromosomes that group into 14 triplets — a classic triploid pattern — and protein electrophoresis fit a triploid dosage. In 1991, Ota and colleagues found the same karyotype in Ryukyu and Taiwan samples, and measured clutch sizes of one to eight eggs that rose with body length.
  • Modern genomes. In 2022, Khedkar, Kambayashi and colleagues published the first draft genome (~1.86 billion base pairs; about 23,560 protein-coding genes) and again described the species as the only known obligate parthenogenetic snake. In 2025, Lv, Wu, Li and colleagues assembled a chromosome-level genome with three subgenomes (A, B and C) that likely diverged around 41 million years ago, and argued that premeiotic endoreplication — copying chromosomes before meiosis so sister chromatids can pair — is the most plausible way the snake keeps its chromosome number while cloning itself.

In plain terms: the snake's cells carry three related chromosome sets from ancient hybridisation. By duplicating chromosomes first, the ovary can pair matching copies and produce eggs that rebuild a full three-set genome — genetic daughters of the mother.

Why does that help it spread?

A single female can found a new population. She does not need a mate after a long trip in a shipping container or nursery truck. That, plus a tiny fossorial body that hides in soil, explains why flowerpot snakes turn up in Florida, Hawaii, California, Mexico, Mediterranean islands and far beyond their South Asian homeland. Harmless to people and pets, they are more a curiosity for gardeners than a danger. Are other snakes ever born without fathers?

Rare "virgin births" have been reported in captive females of a few other snake species after long isolation, but those are sporadic events, not a whole species that has abandoned mating. The Brahminy blind snake is different: parthenogenesis is its normal lifestyle. That is why textbooks and genome papers single it out. FAQ

Can snakes reproduce without a male?

One species can as its normal lifestyle: the Brahminy blind snake (Indotyphlops braminus). Females lay eggs that develop into more females without mating. Other snakes almost always need a mate; rare captive virgin births are exceptions, not the rule.

What is the flowerpot snake?

It is another common name for the Brahminy blind snake. The nickname comes from the way the tiny snakes hitch rides in the soil of ornamental plants and turn up in gardens and nurseries worldwide.

Is the Brahminy blind snake dangerous?

No. It is harmless to people. It is a burrower that eats ants and termites, and it is often mistaken for an earthworm.

How many chromosomes does it have?

Classic karyotype studies count 42 chromosomes arranged as 14 triplets — a triploid set. Newer chromosome-level assemblies recount the same total while showing three ancestral subgenomes.

How many eggs does it lay?

In East Asian samples studied by Ota and colleagues, clutch size ranged from one to eight eggs and increased with the mother's body length.
