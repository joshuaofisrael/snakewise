How Do Snakes Swallow Prey Larger Than Their Heads?

Snakes swallow prey wider than their heads because their skull bones are loosely linked, not because their jaws dislocate. The two halves of the lower jaw are joined by an elastic ligament, the jaw joints swing outward, and each side of the jaw ratchets forward over the prey in turn.

The short answer: flexible jaws, not dislocated ones

It is a popular myth that snakes unhinge or dislocate their jaws to eat. The Herpetological Society of Ireland points out that a dislocation is an abnormal separation of bones at a joint, which would injure the snake. What snakes actually have is a skull built from loosely connected, mobile bones and stretchy skin, so the mouth can open far wider than the resting head looks.

Patrick T. Gregory, a biology professor at the University of Victoria quoted by Live Science, describes two key features. First, the two halves of the lower jaw are not fused at the chin as ours are. They are joined by an elastic ligament that lets them spread apart. Second, the quadrate bone, which links the lower jaw to the skull, is not rigidly attached. It articulates with the skull at one end and moves freely, so the back of the jaw can swing outward.

Why the quadrate bone matters most

In a classic X ray film study of the yellow rat snake, Kenneth Kardong (1986) followed marked points on the skull while the snakes swallowed prey. He reported that the joint between the quadrate and the lower jaw advances forward and flares out sideways, away from the prey, which widens the throat opening at the angle of the jaws. Kardong suggested that this sideways flaring may contribute more to the snake's large gape than the free chin ligament on its own.

The Herpetological Society of Ireland adds an important limit. A wide gape does not guarantee that a meal can be swallowed. The largest prey a snake can take is set by the space inside the mouth and throat that the prey has to pass through, and that is ultimately linked to the width of the snake's head.

The pterygoid walk: how the head moves over the meal

Snakes do not so much pull food in as walk their head forward over it. The toothed bones of the upper jaw and palate on each side can move independently of the other side. The Herpetological Society of Ireland describes the sequence as a ratchet. One side moves forward and takes a new grip while the other side holds the prey in place, then they swap. This alternating motion is often called the pterygoid walk, after the pterygoid bones of the palate.

Kardong's film study supports this picture. He wrote that swallowing progress in the rat snake came more from the jaws advancing along the prey than from pulling the prey into the mouth, and that neck muscle contractions take over near the end to finish the job.

Head first, then muscles do the rest

According to the Herpetological Society of Ireland, snakes usually start swallowing once prey has stopped struggling, and they almost always begin at the head. Swallowing head first folds the prey's legs back against its body, so limbs are less likely to snag or tear the snake's gut.

Once the widest part has passed the jaws, waves of muscle contraction along the body push the meal down toward the stomach. Elastic skin lets the neck and body stretch around the bulge.

Live Science describes an extreme case studied by Kate Jackson of the University of Toronto and colleagues: common king snakes swallowing corn snakes at least as long as themselves. On video, the king snakes used the pterygoid walk and also bunched their own spine into concertina like waves, compressing the prey's body so it fitted inside them.

How do snakes breathe while swallowing?

A big meal can take a long time to go down, and the mouth is completely full. The Herpetological Society of Ireland explains that a snake can push its glottis, the opening of the windpipe, forward and out past the prey. It works a bit like a snorkel, so the snake can keep breathing while it swallows.

What this means for pet snakes

Because the limit is the inside of the mouth and throat rather than the gape alone, an oversized meal can still be a problem. If you keep a snake, follow the feeding guidance of a reptile vet and see our pet snake basics. Never try to feed or handle a wild snake.

FAQ

Do snakes dislocate their jaws to eat?

No. Their lower jaw halves are joined by an elastic ligament and the quadrate bone moves freely, so the jaws spread and swing without any dislocation.

How can a snake swallow something wider than its head?

The loosely linked skull bones, the outward flaring of the jaw joints and stretchy skin let the mouth and throat open far wider than the resting head. Each side of the jaw then ratchets forward over the prey.

Why do snakes swallow prey head first?

Starting at the head folds the prey's legs against its body, which makes it easier to swallow and less likely to injure the snake's gut.

How do snakes breathe while eating a large meal?

They can extend the glottis, the opening to the windpipe, out past the prey so they can keep breathing.

Is there a limit to how big a meal can be?

Yes. The space inside the mouth and throat that the prey must pass through sets the limit, and that is linked to the width of the snake's head.
