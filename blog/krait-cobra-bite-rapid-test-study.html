Study: A Rapid Strip Test to Tell Krait Bites From Cobra Bites

Danish and Thai researchers have built a prototype strip test that spots a krait-only neurotoxin, which could one day help doctors pick the right antivenom. It has only been tested in the lab so far.

If someone is bitten by a snake, call local emergency services or poison control right away. Don't rely on this site. The test described here is a lab prototype and is not available for treating bites.

What did the researchers build?

A team at the Technical University of Denmark, working with Chulalongkorn University in Bangkok and the company VenomAid Diagnostics, published a prototype rapid test in Scientific Reports on 17 October 2026. It is a lateral flow test, the same strip format as a home pregnancy or COVID test, designed to tell krait (Bungarus) envenoming apart from cobra (Naja) envenoming in Southeast Asia.

The strip uses nanobodies, small antibody fragments taken from heavy-chain-only antibodies, that latch onto α-bungarotoxin. The authors describe that neurotoxin as unique to kraits, and they screened their nanobodies so they would not also grab α-cobratoxin from cobra venom.

Why does telling kraits from cobras matter?

The authors note that knowing which snake caused a bite is critical for choosing the right antivenom, yet it remains a major diagnostic challenge. Bite victims often don't see the snake clearly, and both kraits and cobras can cause dangerous neurotoxic envenoming. A quick bedside test could, in principle, help doctors in resource-limited settings give targeted antivenom sooner.

How well did it work?

  • In buffer, and in urine and serum samples that had toxin added in the lab, the strip showed α-bungarotoxin by eye at 0.5 to 5 ng/mL.
  • It detected whole krait venom at 50 to 500 ng/mL in buffer.
  • Cobra venom gave no signal at clinically relevant levels; a signal appeared only at very high concentrations of 50,000 ng/mL or more.

What are the limits?

This was a proof-of-concept study. The samples were commercially bought, anonymised human serum and urine with toxin or venom spiked in, not samples from real snakebite patients. The authors say the results support the feasibility of such tests, so clinical trials would still be needed before anyone relies on one. The paper also discloses that two authors have filed a patent application linked to one of the nanobodies, and some authors are affiliated with VenomAid Diagnostics.
