Study: Removing Large Female Burmese Pythons Has the Biggest Impact

Not every invasive Burmese python caught in Florida has the same effect on the population. A new University of Florida study scores each removed python by how likely it was to survive and breed, finds that large reproductive females count for far more than small snakes, and shows that machine learning can forecast when searches are most likely to remove them.

News · Published 19 October 2026 · Last reviewed 19 October 2026

The news in brief

  • What: a scoring system called the Weighted Removal Index (WRI) that values each captured python by its expected contribution to survival and reproduction, plus models that forecast high-impact removal surveys.
  • Who: Alexander Romer, Melissa Miller, Sergio Balaguera-Reina, Frank Mazzotti and colleagues, led by the Croc Docs team at the UF/IFAS Fort Lauderdale Research and Education Center.
  • Where published: Ecological Applications, first published 21 September 2026, open access under a CC BY 4.0 licence. UF/IFAS announced the work on 28 September 2026, and Phys.org republished the release on 1 October 2026.
  • Data: survey records from the South Florida Water Management District's Python Elimination Program.
  • Main message: count which pythons are removed, not just how many.

Why are Burmese pythons a problem in Florida?

Burmese pythons (Python bivittatus) are not native to Florida, but they have become one of the most significant invasive predators in South Florida. The UF/IFAS release describes a broad diet made up mainly of mammals and birds, including species of conservation concern. Like other pythons, they are non-venomous and kill by constriction (see our venom vs constriction page). They are also hard to find: the researchers note that the snakes are highly cryptic and spend much of their time still, often underwater or hidden, so every search takes a lot of time and effort.

What is the Weighted Removal Index?

Earlier work, Romer explained in the release, "treated all Burmese pythons as contributing equally to the population." The new index changes that. It adds together two weights for each captured snake: one for how likely it was to survive, which rises with body length, and one for its reproductive potential, which depends on its sex and size. In the paper, the survival curve levels off at 95% for pythons longer than 250 cm from snout to vent. Young pythons die at high rates, while mature ones are much more likely to survive and breed, and the release notes that a mature female can lay dozens of eggs in a single clutch.

Romer put it simply: "The idea is that a big female might be worth 100 arbitrary ecological points, while a little snake might be worth five." In other words, catching one large female could do as much for the Everglades as catching many hatchlings.

What did the models find?

The team compared three forecasting approaches: generalised additive models, boosted regression trees and neural networks. According to the paper's abstract:

  • Surveys scored highest during waning moon phases, when surveys were assisted, and in warmer, more humid weather. Scores were also higher on warm days early in the wet season and on cool days in the dry season.
  • The neural network did best on held-out test data, with a balanced accuracy of 0.703 for predicting whether a survey would succeed.
  • Contractor performance varied a lot, and the model's predictions of survey success fell within the upper tail of contractors' capture rates. The authors say this means near-term forecasting "can match the performance of the highest performing contractors."

Weighting also changed which months looked important. In the release, Romer said January and February "don't seem that important" if you only count captures, but they become "a lot more important" once demographic weighting is added, because a bigger share of the snakes caught then are mature males and females.

What could change?

Contracted removal programs often measure success by the number of snakes caught. The researchers suggest the index could eventually help managers shape removal effort and incentives around the snakes most likely to drive population growth. "Every Burmese python removed is a win for the Everglades, but targeting large reproductive females is likely to reduce the population most effectively," said co-author Melissa Miller, adding that the approach is especially valuable at new invasion sites and along the invasion front. This is a decision-support tool proposed by the researchers, not a change in any agency's rules.

What does this mean if you see one?

The removals in this study were made by contractors in a state program. If you live in or visit South Florida and see a large snake, leave it alone, enjoy wildlife from a distance and follow the advice on our snake safety page.
