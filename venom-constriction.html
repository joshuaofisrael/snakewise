Venom vs Constriction

Snakes subdue prey in three ways. Some inject venom through fangs. Constrictors such as boas and pythons coil until the prey's circulation fails. Many other snakes simply grab and swallow. "Venomous" means the toxin is injected. "Poisonous" means it harms you if you eat it or get it on your skin. Most snakes are neither in any practical sense.

By the SnakeWise editorial team · Last reviewed 20 November 2026 · Editorial policy

If you are bitten, call local emergency services or poison control. Do not use this page to decide what to do to the wound.

Venom labels may be wrong. A name, a head shape or a colour is not enough to call a snake safe to handle. If you are unsure, keep away and treat it as dangerous. The safety page has the emergency steps, and the ID guide compares lookalikes.

Venom

Venom is modified saliva, made in glands and delivered through fangs. A single venom is a mixture of proteins. Broad effects that turn up in the literature are on the nervous system (neurotoxic), on blood and clotting (haemotoxic), and on tissue (cytotoxic). Many venoms do more than one of those. The inland taipan page is where this site goes into one venom with the mouse-test numbers and the papers attached. Those numbers are not a ranking of how dangerous a bite is to a person.

The injected-versus-swallowed distinction has a studied exception. Hutchinson and colleagues (2007) showed that the Asian keelback Rhabdophis tigrinus does not make the bufadienolide steroids in its neck glands. The snakes get them from toads they eat. Mothers that already hold a high load can provision their young, and snakes from places without toads lack those steroids in the glands. That is a poison stored from the diet, sitting in a defensive gland, not only a venom pumped through a fang. The abstract was checked 20 November 2026.

Fang types

Elapids such as cobras, mambas and coral snakes have fixed hollow fangs at the front of the mouth. Vipers have long fangs that hinge and fold back. Some rear-fanged colubrids have grooved teeth farther back. This page does not repeat fang lengths. The measurements, and the papers behind them, are in which snake has the longest fangs?

Constriction

Boback and colleagues (2015) measured what happens to anesthetized rats while a boa constricts them. Within 6 seconds, pressure at the femoral artery fell to half of baseline and central venous pressure rose sixfold. By the end of constriction, on average 6.5 minutes, 10 of 11 rats showed cardiac electrical dysfunction. Heart rate fell to nearly half within 60 seconds. The authors' conclusion is that constriction kills by circulatory arrest. The study is boas, rats, and a laboratory. It does not measure every constrictor or every kind of prey, and it does not by itself prove what a python does to a mammal in the field. A constrictor people ask about in the wild is the Burmese python in the Everglades.

Neither

Plenty of snakes have no venom apparatus that matters to their prey and do not constrict. They seize the animal and swallow it. African egg-eating snakes are the clearest case written up here: they swallow a bird egg whole, crack it on bony spikes in the throat, and spit out the shell. The mechanics of a wide gape, which are not the same thing as a dislocated jaw, are in how snakes swallow prey larger than their heads.

Compare three ways snakes deal with prey

  • Inland taipan: a venom measured in mouse tests, with the limits of that measurement spelled out.
  • Gaboon viper: the long hinged fang, not a stronger claim about human deaths.
  • Egg-eating snakes: neither venom nor constriction. The egg is the whole method.
  • Burmese python: a large constrictor, and why the Everglades population is there.
