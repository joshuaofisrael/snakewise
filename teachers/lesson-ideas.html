Snake lesson ideas for K-12

Short, flexible lesson ideas that use snakes to teach life science. Each one lists the grade band and the NGSS performance expectation it can support (codes checked on nextgenscience.org). Adapt them to your class; they are starting points, not full curricula.

Last reviewed: 8 October 2026

What does a snake need to survive?

Grade band: K-2 · NGSS: K-LS1-1 · Time: 30 minutes

  1. Show pictures or a video of snakes eating, drinking and basking in the sun.
  2. Ask: what is the snake doing, and why? Record observations on a chart: food, water, warmth.
  3. Explain that snakes eat other animals, not plants, and that they are ectotherms that warm up using the sun and warm surfaces.
  4. Compare with a plant: plants need water and light but do not eat food. Look for the pattern.

Snake habitat survival argument

Grade band: 3-5 · NGSS: 3-LS4-3 · Time: 45 minutes

  1. Read the habitats page together: rainforests, deserts, grasslands, wetlands, oceans and cold climates.
  2. Groups pick a habitat and list which snake traits help there (for example, night activity and burrowing in deserts).
  3. Discuss why snakes cannot live in Antarctica and how the European adder survives north of the Arctic Circle by brumating.
  4. Each group writes a claim, evidence and reasoning: which snakes survive well, less well, or not at all in their habitat.

Structures with a job

Grade band: 3-5 · NGSS: 4-LS1-1 · Time: 45 minutes

  1. Introduce structure and function with the fact sheet: scales, belly scutes, spectacle, jaw ligament, one long lung.
  2. Students complete worksheet Part A and B.
  3. Each student draws a snake and labels three structures with what they do.
  4. Extension: compare one snake structure with a similar job in another animal.

How a snake senses its world

Grade band: 3-5 · NGSS: 4-LS1-2 · Time: 40 minutes

  1. Explain the forked tongue and Jacobson's organ, heat pits, and sensing vibrations through the jaw and body.
  2. Students build a simple flow model: sense (tongue, heat pit, vibration) → brain → response (follow a scent trail, strike at warm prey, move away).
  3. Groups present their model for one situation, such as finding a mouse in the dark.

Lookalikes and survival

Grade band: 6-8 · NGSS: MS-LS4-4 · Time: 50 minutes

  1. Play Lookalike Lab in pairs and note which harmless snakes resemble dangerous ones (for example, gopher snakes that shake their tails and hiss like rattlesnakes, or milk snakes and coral snakes).
  2. Discuss: if predators avoid snakes that look dangerous, how could a trait like warning-like colours or tail shaking change the chance that an individual survives and reproduces?
  3. Students write an explanation using simple probability language (more likely / less likely to survive).
  4. Safety debrief: these resemblances are exactly why people should never rely on colour or pattern to decide a wild snake is safe.

Reading real research: the gliding snake

Grade band: 9-12 · NGSS: HS-LS4-4 · Time: 1-2 class periods

  1. Read our paradise flying snake article and the abstract of Yeaton et al. (2020) or Socha (2002), listed on our research page.
  2. Identify the evidence: body flattening, a J-loop jump, and aerial undulation that keeps the glide stable.
  3. Students construct an explanation of how natural selection could lead to gliding adaptations in tree-living snakes, separating what the studies measured from what is hypothesis.
  4. Discuss the open question the researchers raise: how wild snakes use gliding day to day has not been studied in detail.
